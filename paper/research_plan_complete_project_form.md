# Research Plan: Three Uncertainty-Aware Hybrid Methods for GBM

This plan describes the final proposed analyses, not a chronological record of trial and error. Earlier exploratory analyses exist in the project files; report that history accurately, and do not treat previously used evaluation cases as new confirmation. Tracks A, B, and C are equally important, use separate datasets and endpoints, and are not fused into a patient-level digital twin.

## Rationale

Glioblastoma varies across patients, within tumors, and during treatment. Each data type has a distinct failure risk: single-cell models can learn donor identity instead of tumor biology; MRI growth parameters can be unstable when scans are sparse and boundaries are uncertain; and simulated treatment policies can appear effective only because their drug-response assumptions are favorable. Prior work already studies donor-held-out annotation and domain generalization (Fischer et al., 2024; Zhong et al., 2024), reaction-diffusion tumor inversion (Subramanian et al., 2020; Zhang et al., 2023), spatially correlated segmentation uncertainty (Monteiro et al., 2020), and model-informed reinforcement learning for GBM therapy (Liu et al., 2025; Aif et al., 2026). Generic use of a graph model, PDE, uncertainty model, or reinforcement learning is not the proposed contribution.

This project develops three separate hybrid methods. Track A combines sparse gene selection, an expression graph, donor-balanced learning, and selective prediction. Track B combines a reaction-diffusion PDE with a probabilistic observation model for segmented MRI masks and a rule that withholds biological parameter claims when the data do not identify them. Track C combines a sensitive/resistant tumor model with a scenario-robust two-threshold adaptive policy and a rule that withholds a preferred policy when rankings change across plausible assumptions. Each full method will be compared with its individual components and strong task-specific baselines. The exact novelty of each combination remains to be established by focused prior-art checks before any novelty claim.

## Research Question and Hypotheses

**Research question:** Do three task-specific hybrid methods improve reliable prediction or decision-making over their component methods and strong baselines when tested on held-out donors, patients, or simulated parameter scenarios?

**Track A hypothesis:** I hypothesize that a sparse, inductive graph classifier trained with worst-donor loss will have lower donor-held-out area under the risk-versus-coverage curve than each one-component ablation and the strongest prespecified classifier baseline.

**Track B hypothesis:** I hypothesize that a reaction-diffusion forecast with a spatially correlated boundary-error model will better predict agreement with the supplied held-out segmentation labels, measured by lower boundary-band Brier score, than deterministic, independent-error, persistence, and volume-trend methods; this tests agreement with the supplied mask, not the true tumor boundary, and separate growth parameters will be reported only if recovery and identifiability gates pass.

**Track C hypothesis:** I hypothesize that a particle-filtered, lower-tail-optimized two-threshold controller will have longer held-out lower-decile simulated time to progression than its component ablations and fixed, adaptive, and PPO baselines under the same observation stream and dose cap; if rankings reverse across prespecified scenario strata, I will report no universal preferred policy.

## Procedures

### Shared procedure

1. Create a source manifest for every dataset and simulation. Record exact release, files, checksums, source labels, identifiers, preprocessing, terms, exclusions, and analysis role before modeling.
2. Keep Track A molecular data, Track B MRI data, Track C simulations, and any clinical association separate. Do not infer a patient-level link across datasets without a verified source crosswalk.
3. Use donor, patient, or virtual parameter scenario as the independent evaluation unit. Fit preprocessing, feature selection, tuning, calibration, and policy selection on training units only.
4. Freeze the primary endpoint, comparison methods, uncertainty method, failure criteria, and data split before scoring the evaluation units. Report every exclusion, failed fit, and non-estimable endpoint.
5. Treat earlier project evaluations as exploratory. Do not reuse a previously examined test group as fresh confirmation by changing the metric or model.

### Track A — donor-robust molecular and spatial prediction

1. Download the UCSC multiomic-gbm files. Inventory assay, expression matrix, cell and spot labels, donor and sample identifiers, measured genes, and file terms. Resolve aliases from source documentation. Do not count unresolved sample IDs as separate donors.
2. Set the primary target to source-labeled GBM Core versus GBM Periphery cells. Exclude cells without a verified donor or target label. Report donor and class counts before model fitting. Keep NormalBrain and other tumor diagnoses outside this target.
3. Build donor-grouped outer evaluation folds. Within each outer training set, use donor-grouped inner folds to choose genes, graph settings, model regularization, and the abstention threshold. Keep every cell from one donor in one fold. Before full nested evaluation, profile one training fold for runtime and peak memory; use another host only if source terms allow it.
4. Fit a sparse-gated softmax classifier with worst-donor cross-entropy, a ridge penalty on classifier weights, and graph smoothness on training-cell probabilities. The ridge penalty prevents classifier weights from growing to cancel shrinking gene gates. Build a weighted 15-nearest-neighbor graph from training-normalized expression. For each held-out cell, blend its classifier probabilities with weighted probabilities from its 15 nearest training cells; select blend weight and temperature inside inner donor folds. Never connect test cells to each other or use donor identity, tissue labels, or held-out labels as model inputs.
5. Compare the full hybrid with three component ablations: (i) set the sparse penalty λ₁ to zero while retaining the ridge penalty; (ii) set graph smoothness λ₂ and neighbor blending γ to zero; and (iii) replace worst-donor loss with cell-weighted empirical loss. Also compare multinomial logistic regression and the existing attention classifier when its inputs match. Keep folds and tuning budgets matched; use a donor-composition-only predictor only as a leakage diagnostic.
6. Rank each cell by its maximum predicted class probability. The primary outcome is donor-averaged area under the risk-versus-coverage curve from 50% to 100% coverage; lower is better. Report calibration and macro-F1 secondarily. Do not claim a formal coverage guarantee unless its assumptions are met. Score outer held-out donors once.
7. Test spatial transfer as a separate endpoint only when source-measured spatial labels, sample identity, measured genes, and reuse terms are documented. Hold out the whole specimen from fitting. Do not use dissociated-cell labels or predicted pseudozones as spatial ground truth.
8. Stop model scoring if donor identity, label meaning, or permitted use cannot be established. Treat failure to beat the strongest baseline, unstable donor rankings, or excessive abstention as a valid result.

### Track B — uncertainty-aware tumor growth and recurrence forecasting

1. Build a patient-by-visit manifest from MU-Glioma-Post. Verify diagnosis, scan date, MRI-to-mask alignment, voxel spacing, tumor labels, treatment context, and duplicate or missing visits. Define one physical tumor-volume rule before analysis.
2. Before patient fitting, simulate at least 100 longitudinal mask series at the observed visit intervals. Create boundary errors by displacing the synthetic surface with a spatial Gaussian field; vary displacement SD over 0, 2, and 4 mm and correlation length over 2, 5, and 10 mm. Include anisotropic and misspecified growth cases. Time five synthetic and five training-patient fits, including uncertainty draws, and record peak memory. If the 2 mm analysis exceeds desktop capacity, use Kaggle only if the exact data terms permit cloud processing; otherwise stop before scoring held-out patients. Require at least three ordered masks for a core forecast; fit on the earliest two and forecast the next, then use rolling-origin forecasts if later scans exist.
3. Use the Fisher-Kolmogorov equation with K=1, initial concentration equal to the first mask (1 inside, 0 outside), no-flux boundary on the baseline-mask bounding box expanded by 30 mm, isotropic D in the primary fit, and visible contour c=0.5. Solve on a 2 mm grid with a 0.05-day explicit time step. Search rho and D on a 21-by-21 logarithmic grid, refine around the best pair on an 11-by-11 grid, and optimize boundary-error parameters with at most 200 L-BFGS-B iterations. Fit the spatially correlated Gaussian likelihood on 256 fixed surface points per follow-up mask; select a 2, 5, or 10 mm correlation length inside training folds. Derive 200 predictive parameter draws from a truncated Laplace approximation, sample correlated displacement at 256 surface anchors, and interpolate to other surface points from the three nearest anchors. Validate interval coverage on synthetic series before interpreting uncertainty.
4. Predict each later scan from earlier scans. Keep every visit from one patient in one outer fold. The primary outcome is mean patient-level Brier score for predicting supplied segmentation labels within a 10 mm band around the held-out mask boundary; lower is better. This measures agreement with that mask, not truth about the tumor edge or measured segmentation uncertainty. Compare the full method with an independent-boundary-error ablation, deterministic PDE, persistence, and a time-gap-aware volume-trend baseline.
5. Measure synthetic parameter recovery, interval coverage, fit failures, and profile-likelihood flatness before patient fitting. If rho or D hits a bound, has a flat profile, or fails recovery, withhold its separate biological interpretation. Report predictive performance only with the stated assumptions and calibration limits.
6. Keep recurrence as an optional, separate RHUH-GBM analysis. From the early postoperative mask, form a PDE score P(x) and distance score exp(−d(x,C)/15 mm), combine them as wP+(1−w)D, and choose w on training patients to maximize recurrence sensitivity at the fixed 15 mm-expanded volume, breaking ties by Dice. On each held-out patient, threshold the highest-risk brain voxels to the volume of the 15 mm physical expansion and compare recurrence sensitivity and Dice with that fixed expansion. Use only the public CC BY 4.0 brain-extracted files; never use the held-out recurrence mask to tune the map.
7. Use LUMIERE only for a secondary sensitivity analysis of the frozen volume-forecast method after its exact file and article terms are reconciled. Do not tune on it or call it a clean external confirmation. Do not interpret fitted growth as untreated biology when scans span treatment. Do not claim clinical validity from retrospective predictions. Stop the affected analysis if scan lineage, visit timing, source terms, or recurrence labels cannot be verified.

### Track C — robust virtual treatment control

1. Use the two-compartment model dS/dt = ρS(1−(S+R)/K)−κuS and dR/dt = ρ(1−c)R(1−(S+R)/K)−εκuR, with K=1, daily decisions, and 0.1-day integration steps. Use inherited virtual ranges \(\rho\in[0.005,0.05]\) day⁻¹, \(\kappa\in[0.02,0.08]\) day⁻¹, initial resistant fraction [0.001,0.1], resistance cost [0,0.25], residual effect [0,0.2], and initial burden fraction [0.08,0.8]; these are assumptions, not patient measurements. Draw rates and initial resistant fraction log-uniformly; draw cost, residual effect, and initial burden uniformly. Use \(\sigma_y=0\) only for an exact-observation simulator check. Evaluate and tune particle-filter policies at positive noise levels \(\sigma_y\in\{0.05,0.10,0.20\}\); generate 256 Latin-hypercube training scenarios and 256 disjoint evaluation scenarios balanced across these three levels, with five noise replicates per scenario. Before full policy search, time the threshold grid on ten training scenarios and a 100,000-step PPO pilot; use Kaggle for these synthetic simulations if desktop resources are insufficient.
2. The proposed hybrid uses a 500-particle filter to estimate hidden resistant fraction from noisy burden and treatment history. Use the Gaussian observation likelihood at positive noise levels. When effective sample size falls below 250, systematically resample and apply one Liu–West parameter-regularization step to standardized static-parameter particles, with shrinkage a=0.98 and kernel variance (1−a²) times their weighted covariance; reflect proposals at prior bounds. This regularizes parameter uncertainty and does not add tumor-state process noise. Define 90% intervals by weighted 5th and 95th particle quantiles. Require 80–97% validation coverage and, across five filter seeds, tail-probability range ≤0.10 at 90% or more of validation decision times before using the posterior to support a controller claim. Give a dose if observed normalized burden exp(y_t−y_0) reaches a∈{0.8,1.0,1.2,1.4,1.6} or if posterior probability of resistant fraction reaching b∈{0.05,0.10,0.20,0.35,0.50} is at least 0.8. Select thresholds on training scenarios to maximize lower-tail 10% mean progression-free time under a dose cap equal to the 5-days-on/23-days-off schedule. Apply the same daily cap to every policy; after B active dose days, force all later actions off. Define simulated progression as burden reaching 1.4 times its running nadir; this is not RANO.
3. Compare with three ablations: replace the particle filter with an extended Kalman filter point estimate, remove the resistance trigger (the burden-only adaptive baseline), and use mean-time rather than lower-tail optimization. Compare also with the fixed schedule and PPO trained on the same simulator and training scenarios. Give PPO the same observed burden, dose history, posterior mean and standard deviation, and posterior probabilities above every b threshold. Use the same dose cap and evaluation scenarios for every arm. Keep any true-state policy as an upper-bound reference only.
4. The primary outcome is held-out lower-decile mean time to the model-defined progression threshold, capped at a horizon frozen from training-only pilot simulations. Select the shortest horizon among 500, 1,000, and 1,500 days for which fewer than 90% of training scenarios remain progression-free under the fixed schedule. Report paired differences, capped fraction, median time, burden integral, resistant fraction, and cumulative dose secondarily. If at least 90% of held-out scenarios are capped, report the endpoint as ceiling-limited.
5. Report a universal policy preference only if the full hybrid beats every prespecified ablation and the strongest baseline on the primary outcome without a rank reversal across the predeclared parameter strata. Otherwise report the scenario-dependent ranking or no preference. Do not call a simulated threshold RANO, clinical progression, or patient response.
6. Analyze MGMT status and progression in MU clinical data only as a separate observational association after verifying the workbook, codebook, time origin, event, censoring, and missingness. Do not use this association to set virtual resistance or infer a treatment effect.

## Risk Assessment

Use de-identified public data under each source’s terms. Keep data on the desktop unless terms allow cloud processing. Do not identify patients or link cohorts without source documentation. This project uses no wet-lab materials or patient contact. Model outputs are research predictions, not medical advice or treatment recommendations.

## Data Analysis

Track A primary: donor-averaged risk-coverage area over 50–100% coverage (lower is better). Track B primary: patient-averaged boundary-band Brier score for the held-out mask (lower is better). Track C primary: held-out lower-decile mean simulated time to progression under the common dose cap (higher is better). Use paired donor-, patient-, or scenario-level uncertainty; do not treat cells or voxels as independent subjects. A hybrid counts as better only if it improves its primary outcome over every one-component ablation and the strongest feasible baseline on held-out units; otherwise state where it loses.
